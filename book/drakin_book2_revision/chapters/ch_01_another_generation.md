Aida looked down upon the sprawling battlefield, white fire pouring from her eyes. Anger, regret, and grief warred across her face as she watched the devastation unfold. The Shree Star, her world's living god, was sinking beneath the horizon, its radiance failing before the ruin below. Soon the plateau would drown in dark.

"This is too much," she murmured. "I never asked for this." Her fists clenched until the Scala on her skin pressed cold against her palms.

She knew the hour was near. When Shree set, blackness would swallow Plymouth, and from the heart of the plateau the Antrolls would boil up out of the deep. Her Drakin would fall from above. Caught between the horde below and the storm of wings overhead, the Avian fledgling armies would break, and their sky fleets and their knights would be annihilated. This was not another skirmish in a war without end. This was the end itself.

Her mind turned, as it always turned now, to the road that had brought two great races to the edge of extinction. She would not pretend she carried no blame. But she also knew the truth beneath the blame: on both sides, speech had failed, and reason had gone deaf. Neither would loosen its grip. Neither would live and let live. That refusal had carried them here, to this last precipice, and there was no lifting her hands now to end it. The belief that one race could only survive upon the corpse of the other had rooted too deep. This battle was the harvest of centuries of hatred — and whatever grew from it would decide the fate of everything that breathed on Cygleshia.

Tears cut down her face. *Should I surrender? Give my life, and buy peace with it?* The thought flickered and died. Her life was not the price on the table. The price was her entire people, every child she had reborn, and she would not pay it. She had not chosen this war. She had been driven to it — driven to free her own from chains and cages and the auction floor. And so her sorrow hardened, the way grief will, into something with an edge.

"Today," she said, and the word carried on the wind, "Avian cruelty ends."

She could not soften it into anything gentler, though she wanted to. She was the mother of a race that had wrung a terrible toll from countless Avian mothers, and she knew it. Drakin blood had bought Drakin survival, drop for drop. The Avians owed her people a debt written in centuries — and tonight she had come to collect. The cost sat heavy in her chest. But the enemy, like her, would pay any price to live. That was the whole tragedy of it: neither side was wrong to want to survive, and both were monstrous in the wanting.

Above the palace, *Vanguard* hung in the smoke — the pride of the Avian fleet, its hull a living Docking Whale hundreds of meters from snout to fluke. The great beast's back was flat plate armor, a landing field for riders; its flanks fell away into open hangars where the Ceraon and their riders waited to launch; and from the crown of its skull a metal plume rose in tiered decks, the bridge crowning the highest. From that height the Avian commanders read the battlefield and marshaled the defense. *Vanguard* was the linchpin, and it knew it, holding station over the royal tree like a shield that would not move.

Below it the whole empire had answered the call. Fleets of armored whales drifted through the smoke, appearing and vanishing, ballistae along their spines loosing bolts the length of a knight that sliced the air and tore into Aida's descending host. Avian warriors met her Drakin in the open sky, wings snapping through hard turns, Scala armor gleaming, and for all her sorrow Aida could not deny the discipline of them. They fought well. They fought for their homes. It would not be enough.

She let her gaze widen over the whole of it, and the world laid itself out beneath her.

The plateau rose thousands of feet above the dark forests of Farasum — Plymouth, one of the great stone kingdoms of the Avian empire. Across all of Cygleshia they stood the same: from the white mountains of the north to the deep southern seas to the parched eastern deserts, vast rocks jutted from the land like the stumps of felled trees, and atop each one a city that took the plateau's name. Roads and rail lines and buildings had been carved straight into the stone, climbing above the surface and burrowing below it, and at Plymouth's center grew a single colossal stone tree, its trunk hollowed into halls, its branches into barracks. There the royal family lived, and there the Mature Avians trained — those permitted to grow their white feathered wings, raised apart and above the wingless fledglings who filled the city beneath them. Sky Whales, Ceraon riders, and Mature knights ringed the tree, the tip of the empire's spear, gathered now to drive back the enemy above and the enemy below at once.

Deep under the surface, water moved. Rivers had risen from the roots of the plateau and cut chasms through the rock over ages, until at last they burst from its walls as waterfalls and spilled down the cliffs into the forests far below. No one knew why the great rocks rose from the face of the world, layer upon patient layer. But everyone knew what waited inside them. The Antrolls dwelt in every dark place of Cygleshia, and they never stopped carving. Level below level, they cut and hauled the stone, hollowing whole cities into the hearts of the plateaus — until, now and then, a fresh tunnel broke through the outer wall and the light of Shree came pouring in to scald their chitin and their black Scala.

They did not sleep. They did not eat or drink. Unfeeling, they toiled in the dark and rose only to kill — and yet they did not consume the dead. They *harvested* them. In the carved undercities that mirrored the bright kingdoms above, black Scala swarmed the corpses, thriving on the bone and blood and flesh, and where the black scales fed, the dead did not stay dead. They were remade. Those buried tombs were the true wombs of the horde: every fallen thing that reached them rose again as a monster, and the Antroll ranks swelled with the empire's own beloved dead.

Even now Aida could not hear the screaming far below, where the Antrolls threw themselves against the underside of the city. But she could see its answer: the mouths of the lower tunnels burning, one after another, set alight by wingless fledglings desperate to hold the deep until nightfall. When Shree fell, the fires would not be enough. The monsters would flood the streets and begin the harvest, as they did every night — pushed back by dawn, returned by dusk, an appetite that had never once been filled.

The Avians of the surface felt her Drakin bearing down like a weight that would finally crush them. It would not. She knew her people would not waver. To the Avians this was the last stand for the survival of their race — their one chance to end the endless slaughter of the Drakin Mother who used their own kind to grow her armies. To them she was a parasite, feeding on their deaths to breed. And they were not wrong about the mechanism, only about the guilt of it: her venom killed an Avian, yes, and in the dying the change began, and the one who woke from it woke bound to her will, their old life gone. She had not asked for that power any more than she had asked for this war. It had been done *to* her — an Avian queen unmade into a Drakin mother — and every account kept in the halls of Preservation traced from that single wound an ocean of blood across Cygleshia's skies.

"No," she said aloud, correcting the despair before it could take her. Her gaze swept the vast bright reach of sky over Plymouth. "This battle is not like the others. This is the last."

There were no winners left to name, and no losers. Everything had already been lost. The Drakin had been hunted like animals, and like cornered animals, they had chosen to fight back. Both sides were long past the point of return, and now — Shree willing — only one race would remain to see the morning. She wished, without any hope in the wish, that it had not come to this. She wanted peace with every fiber she had left. But this war belonged to no single hand. It was the failure of every leader on both sides who could not stop feeding the hatred they themselves had been fed, generation into generation, until the feeding was all that was left.

She looked to her own. Her Drakin Knights wheeled in a vast dark vortex high above the Avian lines, black Airsteel armor drinking the last of the light, their formation so dense it threw a shadow across the plateau. Silver Scala shimmered along every rider — the semi-sentient scales that lived on all of Cygleshia's children, Drakin and Avian alike, deployed now for war. From up here they were beautiful. From up here it was almost possible to forget what they were about to do.

Aida let the beauty last one breath longer. Then the fire in her eyes climbed from her grief and burned it clean.

*Forgive me,* she thought — to whom, she no longer knew. *Or don't. But remember, after tonight, who made it necessary.*

She raised her hand, and her people answered, and the last battle for Cygleshia began.
